\section{Task 4.2}

\subsection{Task 4.2a}

From \autoref{fig:wp4_manifolds_system_wide}, manifolds of the Sun-Mars system do not intersect with Earth's orbit. 
However, the invariant manifolds intersect with the parking orbit around Mars (see \autoref{fig:wp4_manifolds_closeup}).
For this reason, the only feasible transfer trajectory using the invariant manifolds is a transfer from Mars L2 to the parking orbit.

\section{Task 4.6}

This solar-sail transfer is best suited to cargo missions. 
Its propellant-free cruise and reusability make it ideal for repeatedly delivering non-time-critical payloads \parencite{frisbee2002solar,rozhkov2023cyclic}. 
The principal challenge is carrying sufficient chemical propellant for the insertion impulses across multiple cycles, or relying on in-space depots \parencite{smitherman2011space}, which introduce their own constraints.

For scientific missions, an orbiter approach is the most natural fit, with few extra challenges \parencite[sec 6.4]{mcinnes1999book}, similar to already developed missions \parencite{lantoine2024trajectory}. 
Sample return is more ambitious \parencite[sec 6.5]{mcinnes1999book}. 
The required lander substantially increases spacecraft mass, degrading the sail's area-to-mass ratio and hence increasing the required area for equal mass.

Crew transport is poorly suited given the multi-year transfer time and associated radiation exposure \parencite{frisbee2002solar, nasa2023ScienceMars}. 
However, the architecture can pre-position supporting infrastructure and cargo ahead of a crewed mission. 
The key challenge is the long and variable transfer duration, which complicates scheduling resource delivery against a fixed crew-arrival date, with independent launch windows.

Across all three, cyclic cargo delivery is the best current application because it tolerates the slow transfer, unlike the others. The general solar-sail limitations (large deployable structures, attitude control) apply throughout.
